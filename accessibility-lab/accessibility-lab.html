PROJECT OVERVIEW: Accessibility settings like larger text, bold text and increased contrast are used by millions of people every day, but most designers rarely see their own work through those settings until something breaks in a shipped product. Accessibility for Workspace is an interactive tool I designed and built to close that gap, using Material Design 3 as its design system. It puts a sample workspace app, Lumen, on an iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch and desktop browser, lets you switch iOS-style accessibility settings on and off, and shows the result instantly. Live gauges measure whether the screen meets WCAG 2.2 Level A and AA as you go, so the guidelines become something you can see, break and fix rather than a checklist at the end of a project.

TRY ME: The lab is fully interactive :) Change a setting, break the app, or follow one of the use cases. *The app and its data are samples.

Problem

Accessibility usually arrives late in the process, and in a form that's hard to act on:

    • WCAG is written as abstract success criteria, like "1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum)", so it's hard to tell what a guideline actually means for the screen in front of you.
    • Designs are reviewed at default settings. Few teams check what a layout does at the largest text size, with Bold Text on, or in dark mode with Increase Contrast, which is exactly where cut-off text, overlapping elements and low contrast hide.
    • Audits tend to happen at the end, one tool and one rule at a time. Teams get a list of violations instead of an understanding of what caused them.
    • The same product ships on phone, tablet, watch and web, each with different constraints, but accessibility is often tested on only one of them.

Observations

The hardest part of accessibility isn't knowing the rules, it's connecting a rule to a cause and a person. A failed contrast check means little until you see the gray text it refers to, and see who can't read it. So rather than building another checker, I organized the experience around three questions:

    • See it — What does this setting actually do to the interface?
    • Break it — Which common design decision causes this failure?
    • Check it — Does this screen meet Level A and AA right now?

These became the three parts of the layout: accessibility settings on the left, a live device preview in the middle, and the WCAG gauges and checks on the right, all updating together.

Tasks & Actions

01. Put one real app on four devices

Accessibility problems look different on every screen, so the preview isn't a static mockup. Lumen is a small, working workspace app, with meetings, focus tips, team chat and shared files, built to adapt like a real product: a sidebar on iPad and desktop, a tab bar on iPhone, and a focused set of cards on Apple Watch. Switching devices keeps every setting in place, which makes it easy to see that a layout can pass on iPad and fail on iPhone.

02. Mirror real system settings and apply them instantly

The settings use the same names and behavior people know from iOS: Text Size from xSmall to AX5 using Apple's Dynamic Type sizes, Bold Text, Increase Contrast, Button Shapes, On/Off Labels, Reduce Transparency, Smart Invert, color filters and Reduce Motion. I added two reading aids, WCAG text spacing and a hyperlegible font. Every change lands on the device immediately, and at the largest text sizes the layout simplifies to a single column, the way well-built iOS apps do, instead of cutting words off.

03. Measure the screen, don't guess

The gauges aren't a checklist of which switches are on. They're driven by 12 checks that run on the live preview every time something changes: the contrast of every piece of text, the size of every tap target, whether the content scrolls sideways at the current width, and whether every image and control has a name a screen reader can announce. Two stress tests run invisibly in the background, enlarging text to 200% and applying the WCAG spacing values, to catch text that would get cut off. Each check shows its measured value, and selecting a failing check outlines the exact elements causing it in the preview.

04. Make failure something you can learn from

"Break the app" adds 12 common design mistakes, each tied to one WCAG criterion: gray text that's too light, links shown by color alone, images without alt text, icon buttons without labels, fixed-height text boxes, tiny tap targets and more. A brand color picker shows how close familiar colors come to the line. For example, iOS system blue on white measures 4.0:1, just under the 4.5:1 that body text needs. Mistakes only fail where they actually cause harm, so a fixed-width layout fails on iPhone but passes on iPad, which mirrors how these bugs slip through review in real projects.

05. Tell the story through people, not rules

Three use cases walk through the app with a specific person: Rosa, 67, with low vision; Sam, 29, with dyslexia and ADHD; and Dan, 41, with red-green color blindness. Each one has a goal, like joining a meeting, and a short series of steps. Every step turns on a setting they rely on, highlights the part of the screen it affects, and names the WCAG criterion behind it. For Dan, the preview first simulates his color vision, then shows how On/Off Labels and underlined links let him tell switch states and links apart without relying on color.

06. Build it on a real design system

Accessibility holds up best when it lives in the design system rather than in individual screens, so I built both the lab and the sample app on Material Design 3. Color comes from Material's color roles, such as primary, surface and on-surface, chosen so text and controls pass AA in light and dark themes. The type scale, the pill-shaped buttons, the segmented buttons and the switches all follow Material's specs, which meant contrast, target size and focus states only had to be solved once and then held everywhere. Material's switch also shows its state through the thumb's size and position, not only color, which supports the same goal as On/Off Labels.

07. Let people hear the interface, too

A caption under the device shows what VoiceOver would announce for whatever you hover over or tab to, such as "Join, button" or "Meeting reminders, switch button, on". When a mistake removes a label, the caption flags the unlabeled control, so missing names become something you notice in the moment rather than a line in an audit report.

Outcome

Accessibility for Workspace turns WCAG from a list of rules into cause and effect. You change a setting or introduce a mistake, and immediately see what happens to the interface, which person it affects, and which criterion it breaks, on every device the product ships to. Because the checks measure the live screen, the gauges stay honest as you experiment. The lab is also clear about its limits: automated checks cover the 12 criteria a tool can measure, and full conformance still needs manual review, such as keyboard testing and testing with a real screen reader. That makes it a practical starting point for design reviews and for teaching teams why accessibility decisions matter.
